Problem 4.20
==================

a.  Fixing the Sun at the origin, the gravitational potential energy of the Earth is

    .. math::
        V(r)=-\frac{GMm_E}{r}.

    This has the form of the Coulomb potential with `e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)` replaced by `GMm_E`.

b.  Replacing the electron mass in the Bohr radius of hydrogen by `m_E` and the Coulomb coupling constant by `GMm_E` gives

    .. math::
        a_g
        =\frac{\hbar^2}{G M m_E^2}.

    .. exec_code::

        G = 6.67430e-11
        hbar = 1.054571817e-34
        M = 1.98847e30
        m_E = 5.9722e24

        a_g = hbar**2 / (G * M * m_E**2)
        print(f"a_g = {a_g:.6e} m")

c.  The gravitational analogue of the Bohr formula is

    .. math::
        E_n
        =-\frac{m_E}{2\hbar^2}
        \left(GMm_E\right)^2\frac{1}{n^2}
        =-\frac{G^2M^2m_E^3}{2\hbar^2n^2}.

    On the other hand, for a classical circular orbit of radius `r_o`,

    .. math::
        \frac{m_Ev^2}{r_o}
        =\frac{GMm_E}{r_o^2}.

    Therefore, the kinetic and total energies are

    .. math::
        T=\frac{GMm_E}{2r_o},
        \quad
        E_{\mathrm{cl}}
        =T+V
        =-\frac{GMm_E}{2r_o}.

    Setting `E_n=E_{\mathrm{cl}}` and using `a_g=\hbar^2/(GMm_E^2)` gives

    .. math::
        \frac{G^2M^2m_E^3}{2\hbar^2n^2}
        =\frac{GMm_E}{2r_o}
        \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
        n^2=\frac{r_o}{a_g},

    so

    .. math::
        n=\sqrt{\frac{r_o}{a_g}}.

    .. exec_code::

        r_o = 1.495978707e11
        a_g = 2.349410300240807e-138

        n = (r_o / a_g)**0.5
        print(f"n = {n:.6e}")

d.  Introduce the positive constant

    .. math::
        C\coloneqq\frac{G^2M^2m_E^3}{2\hbar^2}.

    Then `E_n=-C/n^2`. The energy emitted in a transition from `n` to `n-1` is

    .. math::
        \begin{aligned}
            \Delta E
            &=E_n-E_{n-1}
            \\
            &=C\left[
                \frac{1}{(n-1)^2}-\frac{1}{n^2}
            \right]
            \\
            &=C\frac{2n-1}{n^2(n-1)^2}
            \\
            &\approx\frac{2C}{n^3}.
        \end{aligned}

    The wavelength of the corresponding radiation is `\lambda=hc/\Delta E`.

    .. exec_code::

        import math

        G = 6.67430e-11
        hbar = 1.054571817e-34
        h = 2.0 * math.pi * hbar
        c = 299792458.0
        M = 1.98847e30
        m_E = 5.9722e24
        r_o = 1.495978707e11
        light_year = 9.460730472e15

        a_g = hbar**2 / (G * M * m_E**2)
        n = math.sqrt(r_o / a_g)
        C = G**2 * M**2 * m_E**3 / (2.0 * hbar**2)
        delta_E = 2.0 * C / n**3
        wavelength = h * c / delta_E

        print(f"Delta E = {delta_E:.6e} J")
        print(f"lambda = {wavelength:.6e} m")
        print(f"lambda = {wavelength / light_year:.6f} light-years")

    The value of one light-year is not a coincidence. For large `n`,

    .. math::
        \Delta E\approx\frac{2C}{n^3}
        =\hbar\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_o^3}}
        =\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{orb}}.

    This is the Bohr correspondence principle: the angular frequency of the transition approaches the classical orbital angular frequency. Therefore,

    .. math::
        \lambda
        =\frac{hc}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{orb}}}
        =\frac{2\pi c}{\omega_{\mathrm{orb}}}
        =cT_{\mathrm{orb}}.

    Since the Earth's orbital period is one year, this wavelength is one light-year.
